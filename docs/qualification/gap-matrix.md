This matrix records the Session 7 opening analysis: what the merged runtime already provides in each control domain, and the specific gaps the qualification campaign must close. Every gap carries its evidence and the shape of its repository-owned fix. The matrix is a working campaign artifact; the campaign is complete when every critical and high entry is implemented and proven, and bounded residual risks are explicitly owned.

Gap totals: 0 critical, 13 high, 27 medium, 21 low. Wave 1 (tamper-evident audit integrity, entries 1 to 7)
is implemented and proven; each closed entry carries a **Closed by** note naming the classes that close it.
Entry 2 is closed **conditionally**: database-level append-only enforcement depends on a privilege that managed
MySQL services withhold by default, so it is a property of the deployment rather than of the release. Its residual
risk, the detection that makes the weaker posture visible, and the operator action that closes it are stated in
the entry itself. No other Wave 1 entry is conditional — tamper evidence holds on every supported server.

Wave 2 (encrypted-field lifecycle, entries 1 to 7) is implemented and proven, and every entry carries its
**Closed by** note. Entry 6 closes as **accepted by design** rather than by new code: the write-only record-secret
property is the control, not the absence of one, and the entry states what an audited reveal would have to carry
if a future integration ever needs stored credentials back. Entry 1 closes with a **bounded, owned residual
risk**: live record columns are re-sealed under the active key, but revision snapshots deliberately are not,
because a revision row is checksummed over its snapshot and rewriting one would be indistinguishable from
tampering. The key ring keeps those envelopes readable, so the consequence is that a retired key must remain
loaded until revision history naming it has aged out of retention — a retention rule, stated in the entry and in
the operator documentation, not an unreadable-data risk. Entries 2 to 5 and 7 close unconditionally.

### Wave 4 outcome

All seven entries are implemented and proven, and each carries its **Closed by** note above. Three of them
closed by fixing something rather than only by testing it, which is the point of insisting the tests run
against a real engine: `DoctrineOutboxStore::buryExhausted()` was serializing every dispatcher at the head
of `claim()`, `DoctrineScheduler::dispatch()` crashed a whole scheduler pass on PostgreSQL the first time
two schedulers met on one occurrence, and the new allocator had to classify PostgreSQL's `55P03` by hand
because DBAL does not. None of that was visible from the SQLite in-memory suite, where the lock clauses
compile to nothing and a failed statement does not poison the transaction.

Entry 1 closes with a **deliberately chosen, bounded guarantee**: gapless-on-commit per counter, not
monotonic. The consequences — a hole where a numbered record is hard-deleted, and serialized creates
within one counter — are stated in the entry and in `docs/business-definitions.md`, together with the
`scope` and `reset` levers that narrow the contention. Entries 4 and 6 close with a **named residual
risk** each: PostgreSQL's `lock_timeout` SQLSTATE is unclassified by DBAL outside the allocator, and
workers still have no in-process reconnect. Entry 7 closes with a stated limit on what a single-process
test can reproduce. Entries 2, 3 and 5 close unconditionally.

Every lock-arbitration assertion that would be vacuous on an engine without row locks skips there with a
named reason rather than passing silently, and the deadlock cycle in entry 4 is built with a second
operating-system process because it cannot exist inside one.

## Failure and chaos behavior

Wave 6 is implemented and proven. Every entry below closes, and each names the drill that closes it.
The theme of this domain was that the recovery story was *designed* and almost entirely unexecuted, so
the standard applied here is that a drill must take something real away: a killed relay process on the
network path to Redis or to the database, a `SIGKILL` sent to a live worker in the middle of a job, a
server-side session termination, a real `SIGALRM` delivered to a handler that would otherwise never
return, and a `SIGKILL` sent to a restore that has already begun publishing targets. Where a genuine
kill is impossible on a given machine — permission bits do not restrain root, a read-only mount needs
privileges an unprivileged runner lacks — the drill skips with that reason stated rather than passing.
Every drill runs unchanged in continuous integration except the restore-interruption drill, which is
operator-run because it needs database dump and restore clients on the host and a disposable database;
its script is `tools/restore-interruption-drill.sh` and it is documented in `docs/operations/backup-restore.md`.

The drills exposed three defects, all fixed here rather than accommodated: the Redis boundary let the
driver's own `RedisException` escape past a wrapper whose documented contract is a `RuntimeException`,
and let a dead server turn a readiness *question* into a raised error; the settings cache turned its own
non-authoritative outage into a failed public read; and the media and export write paths emitted a raw
PHP diagnostic beside the typed refusal they already reported properly. One assumption was corrected
rather than fixed: a severed database *session* does not crash a worker, because the driver closes the
lost connection and the next statement opens a new one — the crash-only path is what happens when the
server is gone, not merely disturbed, and entry 4 states both.

### Wave 7 outcome

Six of the seven entries close and carry a **Closed by** note above. Entry 5 stays open **by decision**,
not by omission, and its note now states what adopting a tracer would actually cost rather than restating
that there is no tracer.

The two high-severity entries are the wave. `config/observability.php` had been a document only an
architecture test read; it is now loaded exactly once, by exactly one class, and everything else —
formatter, level, redaction, required context, metric labels, endpoint exposure — is composed from that
instance. The declaration and the behaviour can no longer disagree, because there is only one of them.

**Residual risk, stated plainly:**

- **Counters are Redis-resident and therefore resettable.** Losing Redis loses the accumulated counts and a
  scrape restarts from a lower value. That is the reset semantics every Prometheus client already has and
  `rate()` handles it, but it does mean the counters are not authoritative for anything — audit records
  and durable rows remain the record of what happened.
- **The 5xx signal has no route breakdown.** `method` and `status` are the only labels, deliberately, so
  "which endpoint is failing" is a log-stream question, not a dashboard one. A route-name label would be
  bounded in principle but extension-contributed routes make the bound operator-controlled rather than
  provable, and it was not worth trading the guarantee for.
- **Export *queue age* is unavailable.** `business_report_export_artifacts` has no `created_at`, so the
  shipped signals are queue depth and expired-artifact count. Closing this properly needs a migration
  adding the column; the generic job-age gauges are the interim proxy.
- **The metrics token is a single shared secret with no rotation window.** It is compared in constant time
  and never logged, but changing it is a restart, and there is no second-token overlap the way the
  extension runtime signing keys have one.
- **The log-level override is process-wide.** Entry 6's original wording wanted per-subsystem control; what
  shipped raises or lowers the whole stream.
- **Nothing yet asserts the alert *expressions* evaluate.** The architecture test proves every documented
  signal has a rule, that each rule names an existing runbook heading, and that each states what it would
  have caught — but no `promtool check rules` runs in CI, because that would add a container image to the
  build for a file no build artefact depends on. If a Prometheus toolchain enters CI for another reason,
  this is the cheapest thing to hang off it.
- **`Worker` and `QueueWorkCommand` were left untouched.** `Worker` has no logger, so a job's settlement
  path still carries its correlation identifier only into the `ExecutionContext`, not into a log line.

Wave 8 (backup, restore, and disaster recovery, entries 1 to 8) is implemented and proven. Entries 1 to

Entry 1 closes by **declaring rather than building**: the shipped tooling takes one kind of backup, a

objective is the backup interval. That is stated as a number (60 minutes on the documented reference

for MariaDB and MySQL, WAL archiving with a physical base backup for PostgreSQL — together with the two
facts that would otherwise be discovered during an incident: `tools/backup.sh` records no binlog
coordinate, and no log covers the filesystem payloads, so a database rolled forward past the snapshot
references media and report-export objects that are not there. Building an application-level imitation
of engine archiving was rejected as a weaker second copy of a mechanism the DBA already operates.

Entry 4 closes for everything a drill can honestly reach and leaves **approval spent state open**: this
release ships no administration surface that creates an approval *rule*, so a drill could only reach a
consumed approval by inserting one with raw SQL, which would pass whether or not the real path works.
Rule administration is the prerequisite; until then the check stays manual and is marked as such in
`docs/operations/backup-restore.md`. The restored installation is also still exercised through
production services and the real console binary rather than over HTTP; the HTTP replay the original fix
shape suggested remains available to a later wave and would add transport coverage, not recoverability
coverage.

Entry 8 closes its documentation half and leaves whole-table parity operator-owned, for a stated
reason: acceptance digests are fixture-scoped by design, whole-database equality rests on the dump plus
its checksum and signature, and an installation that wants independent semantic confirmation should
compare per-table row counts during its own drill against its own data. The entry's claim that audit
rows carry no hash chain or anchors is superseded — Wave 1 added both.

One control deserves to be named because it changed what the drill proves. Every check the acceptance
manifest performed could be satisfied by a restore booted with the wrong keys: identical ciphertext,
identical nonce, identical digests, and a completely unusable installation. The drill therefore stops
comparing after the manifest match and starts using the restored system — it decrypts a `core.secret`
envelope through the production cipher and checks the recovered plaintext, decrypts a restored TOTP
credential through the step-up cipher and passes a live challenge with it, refuses the replay of that
code and of a spent recovery code, signs a restored operator in and denies it the operation it does not
hold, and makes the restored installation materialize its runtime, dispatch a schedule and drain a job
through `bin/kumwe` in fresh processes. A wrong `APP_SECRET` now fails the drill instead of passing it.
